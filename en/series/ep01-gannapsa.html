    The purchasing-intermediary ban: hospitals' hidden supply channel

    University hospitals' purchasing intermediaries are being sold one after another. Is the law closing the channel, or just changing its owner?

    Home / Series / The purchasing-intermediary ban: hospitals' hidden supply channel

    Key points first

    • A purchasing intermediary (gannapsa) is a middleman that delivers medical supplies to hospitals on behalf of manufacturers.
    • The Medical Devices Act amended in December 2025 bans sales to hospitals by related-party dealers. It takes effect on Dec 31, 2027.
    • Related-party test: founder's relatives within the second degree, more than 50% ownership, or controlling influence over management.
    • Hospitals are selling their stakes in intermediaries before it takes effect. The channel is not closing; its owners are changing.

    The way big hospitals buy supplies is changing.

    This year, university hospitals' purchasing intermediaries are being sold one after another.

    Here is why, and how it looks from the operating room.

    From the stock chart
    01

    Stents, catheters, artificial joints and surgical tools used in big hospitals' operating rooms mostly do not go straight from the manufacturer to the hospital.

    02

    They pass through an indirect supplier called a gannapsa, or purchasing intermediary.

    03

    Each hospital effectively has one designated intermediary, and manufacturers or distributors must go through that door to get products in.

    04

    The problem was that the main shareholder of the intermediary was often the hospital foundation or relatives of the hospital's owner.

    05

    So when the hospital bought supplies, people on the hospital's side took the middle margin.

    06

    The industry put that cut at 10–20%, sometimes as much as 40%, and called it a 'toll'.

    07

    The amended Medical Devices Act, passed by the National Assembly in December 2025, blocks this structure.

    08

    Device dealers may not sell to hospitals with which they have a 'related-party' relationship. Routing sales through another company to get around it is also banned.

    09

    There are three related-party tests: relatives of the hospital founder within the second degree, ownership of more than 50%, or controlling influence over management regardless of ownership.

    10

    Violations can lead to criminal penalties. The law takes effect on Dec 31, 2027.

    11

    So hospitals are selling their stakes in intermediaries before then.

    12

    Severance Hospital's intermediary, Avison Care, went in May to Ezmedicom, which is controlled by Daewoong Pharmaceutical's owner family.

    13

    Two intermediaries linked to the Catholic Medical Center went in July to Serveone, a private-equity-owned company.

    14

    Asan Medical Center has also put 51% of its intermediary, Medigood Partners, up for sale.

    15

    One number captures the structure. Avison Care reportedly made KRW 21.6bn in operating profit on KRW 22.9bn of revenue last fiscal year.

    16

    A company that keeps more than 90% of its revenue as profit is less a logistics firm than a fee window.

    From the patient chart
    17

    Surgeons choose which materials to use, but rarely know what they cost or whose hands they pass through.

    18

    All a doctor sees is the hospital's approved materials list and what is laid out on the day of surgery.

    19

    Even if you want to try a new instrument, it cannot reach the operating room unless it is on the hospital's purchasing list and the intermediary's route.

    20

    So for doctors, the intermediary problem is a problem of choice before it is a problem of price.

    21

    When the middle cut is large, big multinationals can absorb it, but small Korean manufacturers and distributors with thin margins give out first.

    22

    Even more so when payment is delayed by months. That is why the amended law also requires payment within six months, with interest on late payment.

    23

    News reports name only university hospitals, but this is not just their story.

    24

    At the spine and joint specialty hospitals I have seen, the intermediary was often a company run by an acquaintance on the hospital's side.

    25

    But the law targets relatives, ownership and control. A company owned and run separately by a close acquaintance may not be covered on paper.

    Overlaying the two charts
    26

    The law does not abolish the intermediary channel. If the hospital side cuts its stake below 50%, business can continue.

    27

    What is happening now is not the closing of the channel but a change of owner.

    28

    Hospital foundations cash out by selling their stakes; private equity and large distributors gain university hospitals as reliable customers.

    29

    On this trend, some 80 intermediaries nationwide are likely to consolidate into a few large operators.

    30

    If they become companies that earn money from purchasing, logistics and inventory services, like US group purchasing organizations (GPOs), both hospitals and manufacturers could gain.

    31

    On the other hand, bigger intermediaries become more powerful buyers in dealings with manufacturers.

    32

    People in the field also point out that the rules could be dodged by adjusting stakes or by hospitals using each other's intermediaries.

    What to watch

    1. Who ends up with Asan Medical Center's Medigood Partners
    2. Whether larger intermediaries add logistics and inventory services or simply keep collecting fees
    3. How well the enforcement decree and the health ministry's audits stop workaround deals
    4. Whether small Korean device makers actually get paid faster
    In one line

    The law changes the channel's owner rather than abolishing it. Whether the 'toll' is gone will show not in the news but in whether the shelves in the operating room carry a wider range of supplies.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a purchasing intermediary (gannapsa)?

    An indirect supplier that receives medical supplies from manufacturers or distributors and delivers them to hospitals on their behalf. Many hospitals effectively had one designated intermediary.

    When does the intermediary ban take effect?

    The amended Medical Devices Act, passed in December 2025, takes effect on Dec 31, 2027.

    Will intermediaries disappear once the law takes effect?

    No. The law blocks related-party deals, such as those involving the founder's relatives or more than 50% ownership. If the hospital side lowers its stake, business can continue, so what is happening now is a change of owner rather than the closing of the channel.
