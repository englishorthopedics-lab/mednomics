    KRW 148 in dividends per KRW 100 of drugs: were payouts to doctors' families kickbacks?

    A drug wholesaler 77.5% owned by family members of client hospital directors. The tax office saw the dividends as rebates; the Tax Tribunal saw ordinary dividends paid by share count. It was a tax ruling, not a clean bill of health.

    Home / Series / KRW 148 in dividends per KRW 100 of drugs: were payouts to doctors' families kickbacks?

    Key points first

    • About 77.5% of a drug wholesaler's shares (57 people) were held by spouses and children of directors of 15 client hospitals. Since 2013 it paid out 49.6% of net profit on average.
    • The tax office treated the dividends as rebates received by nine doctors (other income) and issued a notice of income reclassification.
    • The Tax Tribunal (2025-Seo-3143, late October 2026) overturned it in full. Dividends ranged from KRW 4 to KRW 148 per KRW 100 of drug purchases, not in proportion to prescriptions, and 58% of revenue came from ordinary customers.
    • The ruling concerns the tax assessment only, not whether the Medical Service Act or the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act was broken. In 2025, rebates paid as dividends through a shell company led to criminal charges.

    There was a drug wholesaler whose shares were more than three-quarters owned by spouses and children of directors of its client hospitals.

    The tax office saw the dividends as payment for using its drugs, in other words rebates. The Tax Tribunal ruled the opposite.

    We separate how the two bodies read the same numbers so differently.

    From the stock chart: a wholesaler that pays big dividends
    01

    It is a wholesaler that supplies prescription drugs and medical devices to hospitals and pharmacies. The CEO started it as a sole proprietorship in 2001 and incorporated it in 2007.

    02

    From 2010, the CEO began selling shares to client hospital directors and their families. By the end of that year his side held only 20%; the other 80% had gone to the directors' side.

    03

    Revenue roughly tripled the following year and kept growing after that.

    04

    At the end of 2022, the shareholder register listed 57 spouses and children of client doctors, holding about 77.5% between them and linked to 15 hospitals. The company has more than 400 customers in all.

    05

    Since 2013 the company paid out 49.6% of net profit on average every year, reportedly two to three times the average for listed companies.

    06

    The case begins with a company that returned half of what it earned to shareholders every year, most of whom were family members of client hospital directors.

    From the clinic: between prescriptions and dividends
    07

    In August 2024 the Seoul Regional Tax Office audited the company and found a profit-and-loss file that broke out profit by hospital for just the 15 hospitals and their nearby pharmacies.

    08

    The structure the tax office drew was simple: doctors prescribe, the company earns, and part of the earnings goes to doctors' families as 'dividends'.

    09

    The Medical Service Act and the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act ban both giving and receiving money in return for using drugs. So the tax office concluded that families had been made shareholders and the payments wrapped as dividends.

    10

    Its evidence was timing: families bought shares around when a hospital started doing business and sold them before the register closed in the year after the business ended.

    11

    It also pointed to stake adjustments. One hospital that brought the company less profit saw its stake cut from 5% to 2.5%. By the tax office's math, 20–50% of the profit generated by prescriptions in 2021 went back as dividends.

    12

    Other evidence included a shareholder's statement that the CEO introduced buyers and sellers, and share prices below the tax-law valuation. In 2023 a second company with a similar structure was set up, with minor children of client directors among its shareholders.

    13

    The tax office treated the dividends as rebates received by nine doctors, classed as other income for tax purposes, and issued a notice of income reclassification ordering the company to withhold tax.

    Overlaying the two charts: dividends followed share counts
    14

    The company's rebuttal started with arithmetic. If these were rebates, hospitals that used more drugs should get more and those that used fewer should get less. At the very least, you cannot give back more than the drugs you sold.

    15

    The actual dividends did not fit. Taking a hospital's drug purchases as KRW 100, one director's family received KRW 4 in dividends and another KRW 148. Some received more in dividends than the hospital spent on drugs.

    16

    Dividends are paid by the number of shares held, not by prescriptions. So the company argued this money was dividends, not rebates.

    17

    Shareholders said they bought shares with their own money and paid gift tax where the money came from parents or spouses. Some hospitals' families bought shares as long as five years and ten months after business began.

    18

    The company also argued that profit analysis by customer is something every wholesaler does, and noted that the tax office changed its legal reasoning between the objection and the appeal stages.

    19

    In late October 2026, the Tax Tribunal (2025-Seo-3143) overturned the tax office's notice in full.

    20

    The tribunal's test: to treat a transaction as a sham, the tax office must prove it was arranged to avoid tax. A rebate requires a visible causal link, 'paid because they prescribed', but the dividends were paid by share count regardless of prescriptions or sales.

    21

    58% of the company's revenue and gross profit came from ordinary customers, not hospitals or pharmacies linked to shareholders. Nor was there evidence that profit had been channeled to particular shareholders.

    22

    So it ruled that labeling the payments rebates simply because the shareholders were doctors' families stretched the substance-over-form principle too far.

    Same dividends, different conclusions
    23

    This ruling is about whether the tax assessment was right. It did not decide whether the Medical Service Act or the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act was broken. Criminal proceedings run separately.

    24

    Under the dual-punishment rule introduced in 2010, doctors who receive rebates are punished as well as those who give them. Recipients face up to three years in prison or a fine of up to KRW 30m, and license suspension.

    25

    Dividends have actually been prosecuted as rebates. In August 2025 the Seoul Western District Prosecutors' Office indicted a wholesaler that set up a shell company, gave shares to the family of a general hospital's chair and passed about KRW 5bn through dividends and other payments: KRW 3.4bn as dividends and about KRW 1.6bn as fake salaries, corporate cards and the like.

    26

    The difference between the two cases is the substance of the company and the flow of money. The Western District case involved a shell set up to pay rebates, plus bid rigging. This company does real business with more than 400 customers, and over half its profit came from sources unrelated to shareholders.

    27

    The Pharmaceutical Affairs Act bars a wholesaler effectively controlled by a hospital founder or related party from selling drugs to that hospital. The rule touches the hospital–distributor structure in EP.02. Here, families from 15 hospitals split the shares, so no single hospital controlled the company.

    28

    The tribunal did not say this structure is fine. It means that tying client hospital directors' families in as shareholders was not proven to be a rebate by tax standards.

    29

    The rebate debate never ends because, critics say, the cost ends up in drug prices and the insurance fund. Whether the same money is a dividend or a payment will have to be argued in numbers, case by case.

    What to watch

    1. Whether investigators or health authorities look separately at wholesalers owned by client hospital directors' families
    2. The court's ruling in the Seoul Western District shell-company dividend rebate case
    3. Business and dividends at the similar company set up in 2023
    4. What standard the tax office applies to substance-over-form in similar cases
    5. Debate over amending the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act as big hospitals' drug supply concentrates in a few wholesalers
    In one line

    For a dividend to be a rebate, the line 'paid because they prescribed' has to connect. Here, the line broke at the share count. Still, it was a tax ruling, not a clean bill of health.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is it a rebate if a doctor's family owns shares in a wholesaler the hospital buys from?

    This Tax Tribunal ruling held that owning shares and receiving dividends is not enough on its own to call it a rebate. But it concerns the tax assessment only; whether the Medical Service Act or Pharmaceutical Affairs Act was broken is judged separately on the facts. Individual cases need legal advice.

    Why did the Tax Tribunal decide the dividends were not rebates?

    Dividends were paid by share count regardless of prescriptions or sales, and 58% of revenue and gross profit came from ordinary customers not linked to shareholders. Dividends per KRW 100 of drug purchases ranged from KRW 4 to KRW 148 by hospital, so they were not proportional to prescriptions.

    What penalties do doctors face for accepting rebates?

    Since the dual-punishment rule took effect in 2010, doctors who accept rebates can also be punished: up to three years in prison or a fine of up to KRW 30m, plus license suspension.
