    Doctors leaving the operating room: from plastic surgery to dermatology, and the on-call crunch

    In Apgujeong and Sinsa, plastic surgery revenue fell while dermatology and urology rose. Doctors' career paths are shifting. The link to the hospital on-call hiring crunch is still a hypothesis.

    Home / Series / Doctors leaving the operating room: from plastic surgery to dermatology, and the on-call crunch

    Key points first

    • July 2026 monthly revenue per clinic in Apgujeong: plastic surgery KRW 149.8m (-1.3%), dermatology and urology KRW 222.27m (+6.2%). In Sinsa, plastic surgery -10.3%, dermatology and urology +15.6%.
    • Last year 1.313 million foreign patients came for dermatology (62.9%) and 233,000 for plastic surgery (11.2%).
    • Of 3,319 solo clinics in Seoul advertising dermatology, 385 (11.6%) have a board-certified dermatologist.
    • Local revenue is clinic revenue, not doctors' income. The link to the hospital on-call crunch is a hypothesis with no official data.

    Reports say more board-certified plastic surgeons are moving into dermatology procedures.

    In the field, we also often hear how hard it is for general hospitals to find on-call doctors.

    We check whether the two stories connect, separating what the numbers confirm from what they don't.

    From the stock chart: where the money goes
    01

    Local business data from the Small Enterprise and Market Service show 88 plastic surgery clinics in Apgujeong-dong as of July 2026, down 4.3% from a year earlier. Average monthly revenue per clinic was KRW 149.8m, down 1.3%.

    02

    Dermatology and urology clinics in Apgujeong-dong numbered 80, up 6.7%, with average monthly revenue of KRW 222.27m, up 6.2%.

    03

    Sinsa-dong is clearer still. Plastic surgery clinics held at 157 but revenue fell 10.3% to KRW 195.75m. Dermatology and urology clinics rose 8.1% to 93, and revenue rose 15.6% to KRW 302.03m.

    04

    This is clinic revenue, not doctors' income. It is before rent, payroll, equipment and advertising. These statistics alone cannot tell you what one doctor earns.

    05

    But the direction is clear. Aesthetic medicine, once centered on cosmetic surgery, is shifting to non-surgical procedures such as Botox, fillers, lasers and lifting, which the press calls 'petite' cosmetic procedures.

    06

    The nature of demand has changed too. Double-eyelid and nose surgery is one-off, but skin care is repeated every few months. Analysts say regular care by middle-aged customers supports the market even as the population in their 20s and 30s shrinks.

    07

    Most of these procedures are uninsured, so clinics set the prices. High patient turnover and repeat bookings make revenue easy to predict. That is what draws doctors.

    08

    There are dispute numbers too. Last year the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency received 175 mediation or arbitration requests involving plastic surgery and 114 involving dermatology. Surgery carries the burden that an accident can mean permanent disability and large damages.

    From the patient chart: foreign patients
    09

    Health ministry data show 1.313 million foreign patients received dermatology care in Korea last year, 62.9% of all visits by specialty. Plastic surgery drew 233,000 (11.2%); dermatology was more than five times larger.

    10

    Korea Tourism Organization data show dermatology's share of foreign tourists' medical spending rose from 53.72% to 55.72% in January–September this year, while plastic surgery fell from 24.53% to 19.35%.

    11

    Many dermatologists I know opened in Apgujeong and see foreign patients. That points the same way as the numbers above.

    12

    Plastic surgery has not disappeared. A plastic surgeon I know told me his main patients are celebrities from Thailand and elsewhere in Southeast Asia. Where foreign demand is strong, there is still room for cosmetic surgery.

    13

    There are 3,319 solo clinics in Seoul registered with dermatology as a specialty offered. HIRA data show only 385 (11.6%) have a board-certified dermatologist. The rest are run by general practitioners, family physicians, plastic surgeons and other specialists.

    14

    Becoming a board-certified plastic surgeon takes at least five years of surgical training: one year of internship and four of residency. The core of the reports is that more people are leaving those five years behind for procedure-based practice. One clinic in Seocho-dong was reported to have four plastic surgeons working as directors.

    Overlaying the two charts: hospital on-call duty
    15

    General hospitals often say it is hard to find on-call doctors these days, and the reason frequently given is that doctors have moved into skin and aesthetics.

    16

    That part is not proven by these reports and statistics. We could not find official data directly linking the number of doctors who moved to dermatology with gaps in hospital on-call cover. It is a perception in the field and a hypothesis.

    17

    The logic is understandable, though. On-call work means nights awake, while procedure-based outpatient work ends in the daytime and runs on bookings. With two options under the same license, doctors compare both time and income.

    18

    When hospitals cannot find staff, they must raise on-call pay or cut night services. That cost lands on hospital profitability and on capacity for emergency and night care.

    19

    The debate over doctors flowing from essential care into aesthetics is long-running. But it is tangled up with medical school quotas, fees and working conditions, so no single number settles it.

    20

    One thing remains for patients: most clinics with a dermatology sign do not have a board-certified dermatologist. You can check which specialty a doctor is certified in before a procedure.

    What to watch

    1. Next year's clinic counts and revenue for plastic surgery versus dermatology and urology in Apgujeong and Sinsa
    2. How the shares of dermatology and plastic surgery change in foreign patient statistics
    3. Whether official data emerge that directly show general hospitals' on-call staffing shortage
    4. Debate over rules on how clinics advertising dermatology display their specialty
    5. Whether procedure-related disputes rise along with the number of clinics
    In one line

    Money in aesthetic medicine is moving from surgery to procedures, from one-off to subscription-like repeat visits. Whether this links to gaps in hospital on-call cover has no numbers to prove it yet.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why are plastic surgeons moving to dermatology?

    According to reports, procedures such as Botox, fillers and lasers need less in facilities and staff than surgery, have higher patient turnover and generate repeat revenue. Lower liability for surgical accidents is also cited.

    Are all clinics with a dermatology sign run by dermatologists?

    No. Of 3,319 solo clinics in Seoul registered with dermatology as a specialty, 385 (11.6%) had a board-certified dermatologist.

    Is the on-call hiring crunch in general hospitals caused by doctors leaving for aesthetics?

    People in the field say so, but we could not find official data directly linking the two. It is still a hypothesis.
