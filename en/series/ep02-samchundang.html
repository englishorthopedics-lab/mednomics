    How a hospital grew a drugmaker: Sungsim Hospital and Samchundang Pharm

    A drugmaker grew out of a hospital founded by one doctor. The market is now pricing whether that drugmaker can grow without the hospital.

    Home / Series / How a hospital grew a drugmaker: Sungsim Hospital and Samchundang Pharm

    Key points first

    • Chairman Yoon Dae-in, second son of Sungsim Hospital founder Dr. Yoon Duk-sun, bought Samchundang Pharm in 1986, creating a hospital–distributor–drugmaker structure.
    • Unlisted Soha is Samchundang's largest shareholder (about 31%). Soha is owned 56.52% by Chairman Yoon Dae-in and 43.48% by Insan MTS.
    • Samchundang's share price rose from about KRW 230,000 at the end of 2025 to over KRW 1m in three months, yet its 2025 operating profit was KRW 8.5bn.
    • The exchange designated it an unfaithful-disclosure company for failing to file its earnings guidance (5 penalty points).

    Last time we looked at the channel hospitals buy through: purchasing intermediaries.

    This time we see how that channel grew into a drugmaker within one family.

    The classic case is Kangdong Sacred Heart (Sungsim) Hospital and Samchundang Pharm.

    Roots: hospitals founded by one doctor
    01

    The Sungsim group traces back to surgeon Dr. Yoon Duk-sun, who set up the Sungsim Medical Foundation in 1974 and the Ilsong educational foundation in 1982.

    02

    He founded Kangnam, Chuncheon and Kangdong Sacred Heart hospitals in turn and is regarded as the doctor who founded the most hospitals in Korea.

    03

    The hospitals were passed down along two lines.

    04

    The eldest son, Yoon Dae-won, ran the Ilsong foundation from 1989, leading the Sacred Heart hospitals of the Hallym University Medical Center. He died in 2024.

    05

    The second son, Chairman Yoon Dae-in, inherited the Sungsim Medical Foundation and Kangdong Sacred Heart Hospital.

    06

    In 1986 he bought Samchundang Pharm and built a business that supplies drugs to the foundation's hospitals.

    Trunk: a drugmaker grown beside the hospital
    07

    At the center is an unlisted company called Soha.

    08

    Soha was reported in the early 2000s as a supplier of drugs to the Hallym University Medical Center. Its 2004 revenue was KRW 88.1bn.

    09

    Soha was also Samchundang's largest shareholder with 42.5%, and Soha's largest shareholder was Chairman Yoon Dae-in.

    10

    People on the hospital side owned the company that supplied the drugs, and that company in turn owned a drugmaker.

    11

    The skeleton is the same today. Soha is Samchundang's largest shareholder (about 31%); Soha is owned 56.52% by Chairman Yoon Dae-in and 43.48% by Insan MTS, a company wholly owned by his eldest son, Yoon Hee-je.

    12

    Analysts say management sits with CEO Jeon In-seok, the son-in-law, while control rests with father and son.

    13

    A market built on reliable, captive customers is called a 'captive market'. Critics have called it funneling business to affiliates.

    The 2026 stock chart
    14

    This company shook the market this year.

    15

    The share price went from about KRW 230,000 at the end of 2025 to over KRW 1m in three months, making it KOSDAQ's largest company with a KRW 26tn market cap.

    16

    The catalyst was hope around oral insulin (the S-PASS platform) and generic deals for oral diabetes and obesity drugs.

    17

    Yet 2025 revenue was KRW 231.8bn and operating profit KRW 8.5bn. A company with under KRW 10bn in operating profit ranked around 30th by market cap among all listed companies.

    18

    After a US deal was announced in late March without naming the partner, doubts grew and the share price halved within three days.

    19

    The exchange designated it an unfaithful-disclosure company for releasing earnings guidance only in a press release, not a filing (5 penalty points).

    20

    Around the same time, the former CEO planned and then withdrew a KRW 250bn share sale to pay tax on shares gifted by his father-in-law.

    From the patient chart
    21

    To be used in a big hospital, a drug must first pass an internal committee and be added to the hospital formulary.

    22

    Once on the formulary, prescriptions come steadily, and drugs started during a stay tend to continue as outpatient prescriptions after discharge.

    23

    So for drugmakers, a big hospital is not just a customer but the first gate into the market.

    24

    If a company from the same family stands beside that gate, it starts from a different line.

    25

    Still, today's share price cannot be credited to the hospital. What the market is buying is not hospital supply revenue but a future built on oral insulin.

    Overlaying the two charts
    26

    Last episode's intermediary ban comes up again here.

    27

    The amended Medical Devices Act blocks deals with related-party dealers, such as the founder's relatives within the second degree or holders of more than 50%. The Pharmaceutical Affairs Act already has a similar rule for drugs.

    28

    The person who runs a hospital foundation, a distributor owned by him and his son, and a drugmaker whose largest shareholder is that distributor. On structure alone, it resembles what the amended law targets.

    29

    Whether the law actually applies depends on what each company sells to which hospital and how the enforcement decree defines related parties. It is not something to state as fact.

    30

    Still, with the law taking effect at the end of 2027, how the group reorganizes the ownership and dealings of Soha and Insan MTS is worth watching.

    What to watch

    1. European Phase 1 results for oral insulin, and whether Phase 2 is approved
    2. Whether ownership of Soha and Insan MTS and their hospital dealings change
    3. How the arrangement of management by the son-in-law and control by the eldest son is resolved
    4. Whether the company regains market trust after the disclosure controversy
    In one line

    A drugmaker that grew on the reliable customer base of a hospital is now valued on hopes for a single new drug. Its roots are in the patient chart; its share price hangs on a chart of the distant future. The distance between the two charts is this company's risk.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does 'a hospital growing a drugmaker' mean?

    People on the hospital foundation's side own the distributor that supplies drugs, and that distributor becomes the drugmaker's largest shareholder. Since big hospitals are a drugmaker's first gate into the market, the starting line is different.

    Can Samchundang's share price be credited to the hospital?

    No. What the market recently priced was not hospital supply revenue but hopes for oral insulin and generic oral diabetes and obesity drugs. 2025 revenue was KRW 231.8bn and operating profit KRW 8.5bn.

    Does the amended Medical Devices Act apply to this structure?

    It cannot be said for certain. It depends on what each company sells to which hospital and how the enforcement decree defines related parties.
